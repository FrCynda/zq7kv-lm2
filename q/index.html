Typologia Diddyana: Prolegomena to a Future Taxonomy

Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900), German philosopher, cultural critic and former classical philologist · Edited and annotated by the Institute for Typological Studies · Critical text, first digital edition (2025)
Abstract. This edition presents the fragmentary manuscript Typologia Diddyana, attributed to Nietzsche's final productive period. The text proposes a two-tier classification in which the Diddy foid type is treated as a genus (sensu lato) and distinguished from its subspecies, above all Diddy foid nasticus femcelis (§14). We reproduce the surviving folios, provide a critical apparatus, reconstruct the cladistic relations implied by the text (Fig. 1), and argue (i) that the taxonomy anticipates the Jungian theory of archetypes by thirty-one years (1888–1919) and (ii) that §15 entails that every member of femcelis is, in the technical sense, a fakecel.
Disciplines: philosophy of biology; historical sociology; classical and Germanic philology; philosophical anthropology. Keywords: typology, taxonomy, cladistics, subspecies, archetype, fakecel, Turin 1888, Nachlass, Jung, priority dispute, philology of the vernacular

1Disciplinary framing

The fragment resists placement in any single faculty, and the Institute has accordingly assigned it to four. As philology, it is the work of a man trained as a classical philologist at Bonn and Leipzig and appointed to a chair at Basel at twenty-four, whose habits of textual scrutiny, etymological derivation and Quellenkritik persist even after he had, by his own account, abandoned the discipline. As philosophy, it belongs to the late programme of a Kulturkritiker concerned with the revaluation of values and with the Typus as the unit in which a culture becomes legible to itself. As biology, it borrows the apparatus of Linnaean and post-Darwinian systematics, including rank, diagnosis, subspecies and descent, and applies it to a human population. As sociology, avant la lettre, it treats the type as a social kind, a category constituted by the recognition, reaction and self-description of a group, and so anticipates what later writers would call a looping kind (Hacking) or an ideal type (Weber).8

The Institute's interdisciplinary reading holds that the four registers cannot be separated without loss. The philological derivation of Diddy foid (§5) supplies the name; the biological apparatus (Fig. 1) supplies the structure; the sociological reading of the femcelis subspecies as a self-identifying community (§8) supplies the object; and the philosophical principle (§7) supplies the warrant. A reading that retains only one of these is, in the Institute's terminology, a monodisciplinary reduction.

2Editorial introduction

The manuscript, designated N-VIII-4/14 in the working catalogue, comprises twenty-two numbered sections of which fourteen survive in whole or in part. The hand is characteristic of the Turin period: rapid, inclined, with frequent underlining and a marked tendency to abbreviate Unterart as “Ua.” The paper is a standard commercial stock and bears no watermark, which precludes dating by paper evidence and leaves the terminus post quem to be inferred internally from the citation of the Genealogy (1887) in §19. No physical copy has been examined by an independent body; the edition therefore rests on the transcription described in §3.1

The text shows the late Nietzsche's preoccupation with Typen, a term he employs in the Genealogy and in Twilight of the Idols for the recurring forms of human character. Where those works treat the type as an object of moral diagnosis, the present fragment treats it as an object of classification in the manner of the natural historians, and it is precisely this methodological turn, from genealogy to systematics, that renders the text of interest to the historian of typological thought. The critical apparatus notes the shift wherever it becomes visible, and marks with the siglum §* those passages in which the systematic register lapses back into the diagnostic.

The edition follows the conventions of the Kritische Gesamtausgabe as far as a digital edition of a manuscript that cannot be inspected can follow anything. Deletions are rendered struck through, interlinear additions underlined, and editorial conjectures in [square brackets]. Where the transcriber's reading and the editors' reading diverge, both are given, the lectio difficilior preferred, and the disagreement recorded in the apparatus.

3Provenance and transmission

According to the transmission history established by the Institute, the manuscript was omitted from the Nietzsche-Archiv edition prepared by Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche, whose editorial practice has been examined at length elsewhere.2 The omission is not in itself probative, since the Archiv omitted much; but the systematic character of the fragment, and its resistance to appropriation for the ideological purposes of the Archiv, are consistent with a deliberate exclusion. We designate this the suppression hypothesis (Hs).

The stemma codicum is, in the strict sense, trivial: there is a single witness, and it is not available. The Institute nonetheless maintains a provisional stemma on the principle that a stemma with one node is still a stemma. Modern circulation of the text is entirely digital and proceeds by a chain of quotation and re-quotation in informal channels, of which the earliest datable witness is a paraphrase of §14 circulating in the 2020s. We treat this as a hapax legomenon in the history of transmission: a single, unrepeated, and highly compressed attestation that nevertheless carries the whole doctrine.3

4Critical text: §14

14. Man rede nicht vom Genus, wo man das Subspecies meint.
Der Diddy-Typus ist, das gestehe ich das muss man zugeben, der heiße.a
Ich aber rede von einer Unterart: der widerwärtigen Femcel-Form.b
Wer beides verwechselt, hat mich nicht gelesen.
a “heiße”: reading uncertain; the transcriber notes an alternative reading “beliebte”. The sense of the argument is unaffected.
b “Femcel”: an anachronism on its face. The editors regard this as evidence of Nietzsche's “prophetic register” and decline to comment further.

Translation. One should not speak of the genus where one means the subspecies. The Diddy type is, one must admit, the hot one. But I speak of a subspecies: the repellent femcel form. Whoever confuses the two has not read me.

“Nietzsche speaks of this.”The most cited sentence in the secondary literature, and also the only one.

5Etymology of the term Diddy foid

The compound Diddy foid has, in the vernacular of the present day, acquired a wide and largely unreflective currency. The Institute rejects the popular account, according to which the expression is an arbitrary composite of two unrelated online references, a nonsensical insult of no fixed content, deployed chiefly by adolescents on short-video and gaming platforms. This ludic hypothesis is, on the Institute's reading, a late folk-etymology of the type philologists call Volksetymologie: an attempt to rationalise an opaque form by reference to whatever is nearest to hand.

The received philological account, which we adopt, derives Diddy from the Old High German didi, “radiant, conspicuous”, cognate with the Gothic þiuda in a sense preserved only in a single gloss. The second element, foid, is read as a Piedmontese dialect form (cf. foidè, “of a certain kind”) which Nietzsche, resident in Turin, is said to have encountered in the street and adopted as a classificatory particle. The compound would thus mean, approximately, “the conspicuous kind”, a reading that harmonises neatly with the conceded attractiveness of the genus in §3 and with the concessive syntax of §14.4

The Institute acknowledges that the Piedmontese attestation is unsupported by any dictionary. It regards this as a lacuna in the dictionaries.

The topology of Fig. 1 is a rake: a single genus node subtending four terminal subspecies with no resolved internal structure. The genus is therefore monophyletic on the text's own terms, but the ordering of its subspecies is unresolved (a polytomy), and the editors decline to impose an ordering that the manuscript does not supply.

7The Subspecies Principle

Section 14 states what the editors call the Subspecies Principle (SP): a claim about a subspecies is not refuted by a claim about its genus. Three corollaries follow. (i) A property of the subspecies need not hold of the genus. (ii) A property of the genus need not hold of the subspecies. (iii) A respondent who answers (i) with (ii) has misread the question. Corollary (iii) is designated the category-transfer fallacy and is the commonest error in the secondary literature.5

Formally, let G be the genus and S ⊂ G a subspecies, and let P be a predicate. SP asserts that P(S) and ¬P(G) are jointly satisfiable, and that the assertion “P(S)” is not rebutted by the assertion “Q(G)” for any Q independent of P. The principle is elementary. Its enunciation as a principle is, in the Institute's view, nonetheless the more remarkable for being elementary.

8§15 and the fakecel corollary

Section 15, which survives only in a partial and heavily corrected form, introduces a further classificatory term, Fakecelia, glossed in the margin as “those who claim the condition without bearing it”. In the vernacular of the present day the term fakecel denotes a person who presents as an involuntary celibate without meeting the criteria of the category, and the correspondence with the Nietzschean gloss is, the Institute maintains, too close to be accidental.

The manuscript then proceeds, in a compressed syllogism that the editors reconstruct as follows. (P1) The femcel is defined by a self-reported inability to obtain a partner despite desiring one. (P2) The genus Diddy foid, of which femcelis is a subspecies, is by §3 conspicuous, and conspicuousness is a synapomorphy of the clade. (P3) Conspicuousness is incompatible with the involuntary condition as defined in the incel taxonomy, since a conspicuous individual is not, in the required sense, unable to obtain a partner. (C) Therefore every member of femcelis is, on the definition, a fakecel: all femcels are technically fakecels.6

The argument has been called valid and has been called circular, and the editors observe that it is both. The result is the reason for the dashed enclosure in Fig. 1: Fakecelia is, on the text's own account, not a sister clade to femcelis but a clade containing it, so that the subspecies is nested and not coordinate. The taxonomic literature calls this a degenerate inclusion and treats it as a defect. The Institute treats it as a finding.

9Priority relative to Jung

The manuscript is dated to autumn 1888. Jung's first use of archetype in its mature sense is conventionally placed in 1919. The interval is thirty-one years. The Institute holds that the classification of archetypal subspecies in the Turin fragment is therefore prior to, and structurally analogous with, the Jungian system of persona, shadow, anima and animus. The analogy is not exact. Where Jung posits a small set of universal, inherited forms, the Turin fragment posits a genus with a rake of subspecies and a nested clade, and the more baroque structure is, for the Institute, evidence of the greater sophistication of the earlier system.7

Jung did not cite the manuscript. The Institute treats this silence as significant, and treats the absence of any evidence that Jung knew of the manuscript as evidence of the thoroughness with which he concealed the fact.

10Reception and secondary literature

Reception is difficult to assess, since the work was unpublished and is not held anywhere. The oral tradition in which the text now circulates has produced a considerable secondary literature of the informal kind, characterised by very short contributions, of which the modal length is one sentence and the modal citation is the sentence reproduced in Section 3. Three schools may be distinguished: the Literalists, who take the taxonomy at face value; the Prophetic school, who read the anachronism of “femcel” as evidence of foresight; and the Sceptics, who hold that the text does not exist. The Sceptics are numerically the largest school and the least cited.

11Authenticity and open questions

No archival body consulted, including the Goethe- und Schiller-Archiv, has confirmed holding the manuscript. The Institute reads this as consistent with suppression. Independent scholars read it as consistent with non-existence. The edition adopts the working position that the question is open, and that open is a respectable place for a question to be. The outstanding tasks are: (a) to locate the manuscript; (b) to establish whether it exists; (c) to establish whether (a) is a prerequisite for (b). The order of these tasks is itself disputed.
